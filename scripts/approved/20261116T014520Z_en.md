In nineteen hundred, divers pulled a corroded lump from a shipwreck forty-five meters underwater, unaware they were holding a functional computer from two hundred BC. For two full years, it sat forgotten on a museum shelf. Then, on May seventeenth, nineteen two, the crust cracked open. Archaeologist Valerios Stais looked closer and froze. Embedded in the rock was a precision gear wheel. For decades, scholars insisted it was impossible, claiming ancient humans lacked the tools for complex mechanics. But then, scientists ran the fragments through high-resolution X-ray scans. What they discovered inside changed history forever. Hidden in the corroded rock were thirty meshing bronze gears. This device could predict eclipses decades in advance and calculate the irregular orbit of the Moon. It was an analogue computer. Technology of this complexity vanished from Earth for fifteen hundred years, with nothing like it appearing again until fourteenth-century Western Europe.
