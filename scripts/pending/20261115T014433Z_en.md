In 1518, city leaders tried to cure a mysterious outbreak by encouraging hundreds of people to dance themselves free of it. It all started on a narrow cobbled street in Strasbourg when Frau Troffea stepped outside in complete silence. There was no music, yet her body convulsed frantically, eyes vacant and expressionless. Within days, over thirty people joined her, thrashing their arms. Panicked doctors believed their blood was overheated and prescribed continuous dancing as the cure. So the council hired musicians, forcing the victims to keep moving. It backfired horribly. Soon, up to four hundred people were trapped in motion, with up to fifteen dancers collapsing dead every single day from heart attacks and sheer exhaustion. Victims often cried out for help. Dancers were eventually led to Saint Vitus's shrine, wearing red shoes sprinkled with holy water to pray for absolution. By September, the plague vanished. Scientists suspect moldy rye bread, while historians argue mass hysteria, but neither theory fully explains it. If your body suddenly turned against you, would you blame poison... or a curse?
