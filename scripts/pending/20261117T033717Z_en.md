In 1908, night skies across Europe suddenly glowed bright gold—bright enough to read a book at midnight. Thousands of miles away, seismographs in Germany violently shook, while atmospheric waves were detected all across the globe. Yet because the site was hidden deep inside the remote Siberian taiga, no scientific expedition was organized for thirteen long years. It all started at seven in the morning when a blinding light brighter than the sun streaked across the sky. Miles away, people felt intense heat before the shockwave knocked them to the ground. The shockwave flattened millions of trees, crushing over eight hundred square miles of forest in seconds. When explorer Leonid Kulik finally reached the site in 1927, the real mystery began. Right at the exact epicenter, every single tree was still standing—stripped completely bare like silent black poles. Over a century later, not one piece of space rock has ever been recovered. Did a meteor truly vaporize without a trace, or did something else explode?
